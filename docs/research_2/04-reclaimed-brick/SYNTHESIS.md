# SYNTHESIS — Reclaimed Brick Masonry (Strand 04)

Catalonia-based interactive LCA installation. Reclaimed brick is the **BASELINE FLOOR** — the reference axis against which the other three methods (Catalan fired-clay masonry, 3D-printed concrete/earth, prefabricated modular/CLT) are plotted. This strand does not produce a fourth competitor. It produces the floor, plus the methodology-wobble overlay that the installation cannot ship without.

## (a) Evidence landscape

The peer-reviewed literature on reclaimed-brick LCA is younger and thinner than for new masonry, new concrete, or even 3D-printed earth. Three Tier-1 anchors carry most of the weight. Salmio and Huuhka (2026, *Buildings & Cities*) is the most rigorous reclaimed-brick LCA in print: 1-tonne declared unit, two deconstruction methods compared (T1 hand-held demolition hammer; T2 excavator + grapple), full transport sensitivity, and a primary finding that reclaimed bricks deliver 94–95 % GWP-total reduction and 86–88 % GWP-fossil reduction versus new fired bricks under cut-off allocation. Devos et al. (2024, *Journal of Cleaner Production*) is the per-m²-wall companion: 85–86 % environmental gain, deconstruction wastage 30–70 % of mass depending on brick + mortar pairing. The Reclaimed Brick Company UK (2024) third-party verified EPD reports A1–A3 = 20.4 kg CO₂eq/t (versus 250–350 kg CO₂eq/t for new fired brick) and the same product at A–C = −3.18 kg CO₂eq/t when avoided-burden methodology is applied — i.e. one product, two allocation rules, two qualitatively different headline numbers, both verified at primary source.

The two anchor cases the brief required are real and verified. Re:Crete is the EPFL Structural Xploration Lab footbridge built from 25 saw-cut concrete blocks reassembled into a 10-m post-tensioned arch (Devènes, Brütting, Küpfer, Bastien-Masse, Fivet, 2022, *Structures* 43, 1854–1867, DOI 10.1016/j.istruc.2022.07.012). The reported GWP reductions are 71 % vs recycled-concrete-block arch, 63 % vs monolithic recycled-concrete arch, 74 % vs steel-beam arch, and +9 % above glulam timber. Crucially, transport accounts for 34 % of Re:Crete's GWP — meaning the carbon advantage is not absolute, it is conditional. The strand brief's "100 km threshold" claim is in the right ballpark; Salmio & Huuhka measured the threshold more precisely at 315 km (excavator method) to 480 km (hand-held method), but only because Finnish brick is ceramic-only and lighter per piece than Re:Crete's saw-cut concrete blocks.

K.118 / Kopfbau Halle 118 is the second anchor: a three-storey Winterthur extension built by baubüro in situ, opened 2021. Verified figures from the institutional source: 1,266 m² GFA, ~60 % GHG reduction versus equivalent new construction, 500 t primary materials saved, ~70 % component reuse rate, sourcing radius approximately 100–150 km (Basel + Zürich). The De Wolf, Hoxha, Fivet (2020) paper in *Sustainable Cities and Society* (DOI 10.1016/j.scs.2020.102322) used K.118 as its case study to compare six allocation methods (cut-off, end-of-life, PAS-2050 distributed, Environmental Footprint, degressive, SIA 2032). The paper's conclusion is the single most important sentence in this strand: "discrepancies between methods are so high that LCA is not sufficient to measure the environmental quality of reuse practices." That is the strand's licence to put the methodology-wobble overlay on the installation.

The Catalan picture is sparser. ITeC's BEDEC database (2026 edition) has been upgraded to EN 15804+A2 with 22 environmental indicators per element, but it does not yet publish a verified reclaimed-brick (totxo recuperat / ladrillo recuperado) entry. The Catalan government (2021) requires ≥ 5 % recycled-aggregate content in public works, but recycled aggregate is not the same as reused brick — recycled aggregate is crushed C&D waste; reused brick is the brick re-laid intact. IAAC's Material Mining in Barcelona project documents the urban material stock but no operational salvage supply chain. UIA Barcelona 2026 World Capital of Architecture features a "MINERAL — Architects of Urban Mining" challenge whose winning Spolia proposal explicitly recognises the gap. Concular GmbH (Berlin, ~5 hubs across DE) and Restado (DACH, ~1 M items, ~€40 M stock) are the closest functional analogues but operate primarily in German-speaking markets. Catalonia, in 2026, does not have a Halle-118-equivalent supply chain.

## (b) The allocation-rule debate is the load-bearing methodology issue

For new materials the LCA methodology wobbles are real but second-order — system boundaries shift the headline number by ±10–20 %. For reused materials the wobbles are *primary*. The same physical wall, with the same physical bricks, the same physical lime mortar, transported the same 50 km, generates wildly different headline numbers depending on which allocation rule the analyst picks.

Cut-off (the "polluter-pays" or "first-life-pays" convention) assigns 100 % of the original brick production burden to the first-life building and inherits zero upstream burden into the second life. This is what the Reclaimed Brick Company EPD reports for A1–A3: 20.4 kg CO₂eq/t. Cut-off is the most common rule in EPDs because it is the simplest and most defensible auditable accounting. For a 350 kg/m² wall, cut-off yields ≈ 8–25 kg CO₂eq/m² depending on transport, mortar, and brick-yield assumptions.

Avoided-burden (system-expansion in the IPCC / ILCD vernacular) credits the second life with the avoided new-brick production it displaces. This is what generates the *negative* total-lifecycle figure (−3.18 kg CO₂eq/t in the RBC EPD A–C result). It is the most generous to the reused product but is methodologically contested because it can produce nonsensical "carbon negative" walls when in fact a wall of bricks has been physically built. For a 350 kg/m² wall under partial avoided-burden credit, A1–A3-equivalent comes in at ≈ 18–45 kg CO₂eq/m².

System-expansion done as a 50/50 split (PAS-2050 distributed allocation) divides the original production burden equally between the two service lives. ~125–175 kg CO₂eq/t lands in the second life. For a 350 kg/m² wall that is ≈ 35–70 kg CO₂eq/m². This is the most punitive rule for reuse but is what the EU Environmental Footprint method's 50/50 default would assign.

Across these three rules the headline number swings approximately 5×. De Wolf et al. (2020) measured the swing for the K.118 inventory under their six methods and found it large enough to flip the qualitative ranking of reuse-vs-new in some indicators. **The installation cannot show a single number for reclaimed-brick GWP without naming the rule.** This is non-negotiable. The methodology-wobble overlay (Phase 4 of the project plan) must let the visitor toggle the allocation rule and watch the floor of the chart move. Otherwise the installation is presenting a methodology-dependent number as if it were a physical fact.

## (c) Why reclaimed brick is the BASELINE FLOOR, and what that requires of the data

The locked decision in the .planning/ corpus is that reclaimed brick is not a fourth competitor. It is the reference axis. The other three methods are plotted as deltas relative to this floor. There are three reasons this decision is correct.

First, reclaimed brick is the empirical low-bound for embodied carbon at the wall scale. Salmio & Huuhka (2026) and Devos et al. (2024) converge on roughly 85–95 % GWP reduction versus new fired brick under cut-off allocation. No other method available to a Catalan small-building project gets this low. 3D-printed earth approaches it on a thermodynamic basis but requires drying-clay infrastructure that does not yet exist commercially in Catalonia. CLT prefab is biogenic and arguably comparable on lifecycle but exports the carbon question to forestry allocation rules (a different wobble). Reclaimed brick is the *measured* floor.

Second, reclaimed brick already has the methodology wobble baked in — putting it on the floor of the chart forces every other method to declare its own allocation choices in comparison. If the installation puts new masonry on the floor, the other methods can hide behind "we are X % better than new". If reclaimed brick is the floor, every other method has to answer "how much worse than reclaimed?" and that question cannot be answered without naming the allocation rule. The pedagogical leverage is much higher.

Third, the data discipline this requires is explicit. For the other strands the brief asks for low/high ranges + assumption text + Tier. For this strand the brief asks for low/high ranges + **allocation_rule** + assumption text + Tier — the extra column is what makes the floor honest. The VALUES.md table fulfils this with three sibling rows on the structure / co2_kg_per_m2 cell (cut-off, avoided-burden, system-expansion), and the SYNTHESIS narrative names which rule each anchor case used (Re:Crete: cut-off; K.118 + De Wolf: six rules compared; RBC EPD: cut-off A1–A3 + avoided-burden A–C). When the installation reads VALUES.md it should pull all three rows and let the visitor swap between them.

What this requires of the data: every numerical cell must declare its allocation rule, even when "NA" because the parameter is not carbon-related. The labour-hours cell does not have an allocation rule but the "—" placeholder must be present so a downstream parser cannot mistake a missing-allocation-rule cell for a carbon-relevant one.

## (d) Named biases (as specific mechanisms, not "biases may exist")

**Survivorship bias on showcase projects.** Re:Crete and K.118 are the two most-cited reuse precedents in Europe because they are well-documented, well-photographed, and produced by sustainability-active research labs (EPFL SXL) and architects (baubüro in situ) with active publication agendas. Average Catalan reclaimed-brick projects do not get LCAs published. The 60 % GHG reduction of K.118 is achievable when you have a Swiss pension-fund client, a Holcim-Award-winning architect, and a 100–150 km supply chain through Basel and Zürich. A Catalan small project does not have those conditions. The installation should report the Re:Crete and K.118 numbers as upper bounds of achievable performance, not as central estimates.

**Reclaimed-materials advocacy in the source set.** EPFL SXL, ETH Zürich's Chair of Circular Construction, baubüro in situ, and Concular are circular-construction advocates. Their LCA work is methodologically rigorous but the framing is consistently pro-reuse. The strand brief asked for this disclosure; it is named here. The independent triangulation comes from Salmio & Huuhka (a Finnish university group whose paper explicitly tests cement-mortar bricks — a sceptic's material) and from Devos et al. (a parallel European group). The fact that two independent groups converge on 85–95 % GWP reduction reduces the advocacy concern to "advocates report values within the same range as non-advocates", which is the strongest form of bias-resistant data we can get.

**Geographic-transfer bias.** All Tier-1 reclaimed-brick LCA evidence is Finnish, UK, Belgian/Dutch, and Swiss. Catalonia has different climate, different mortar chemistry conventions, different demolition practice, different brick history (Catalan brick is often pre-1950 lime-mortar — favourable for reclaim — but post-1950 cement-mortar buildings are rapidly entering demolition). Salmio & Huuhka transport sensitivity (480 km T1 / 315 km T2) is for Finnish geography; Mediterranean transport emission factors are similar but Catalan grid carbon intensity is currently lower than Finnish, which shifts the threshold. The installation's range cells reflect EU-proxy values; Catalan values are listed as "missing" where appropriate.

**Cost-data absence.** Tier-1 cost data for Catalan reclaimed brick does not exist. Concular and Restado are German-market, Reclaimed Brick Company is UK, US sources are dollar-denominated and a different supply chain. The €95–220/m² range in VALUES.md is a EU proxy with a Tier-2 marker. If the installation needs to claim a defensible Catalan price, an upstream cost study is required.

**End-of-life of the second life.** No published study tracks reclaimed bricks into a third life. The RBC EPD assumes 70 % can be re-reclaimed at end-of-life of the second building, but this is an assumption, not measurement. The installation should not claim infinite reuse cycles; it should state "demonstrated through one reuse, third life unverified".

**Cement-mortar contamination.** Pre-1950 buildings used lime mortar, which is soft and cleans off bricks easily — these buildings yield 60–80 % whole-brick reuse rates. Post-1950 cement-mortar buildings yield 24–38 % depending on deconstruction method (Salmio & Huuhka 2026). Catalonia's accessible demolition stock for the next decade is a mix of both eras; the headline 70 % reuse-rate from K.118 should not be assumed for Catalan supply.

## (e) Brief-revisit: can the installation defensibly say "reclaimed brick produces X kg CO₂/m²"?

**Yes, with allocation-disclosure overlay. Without the overlay, no.**

The defensible single-number claim under cut-off allocation, EU-average transport, Tier-1 sourced data is:

> **Reclaimed brick wall (350 kg/m², new lime mortar, < 50 km transport) ≈ 8–25 kg CO₂eq/m² A1–A3 cradle-to-gate, under cut-off allocation. The same wall under system-expansion or 50/50 distributed allocation reports 35–70 kg CO₂eq/m². The choice of allocation rule moves the number ~5×; this is a methodology choice, not a physical property.**

This is the sentence the installation can put on a wall card. Any short version that drops the second clause is a misstatement.

The installation's methodology-wobble overlay must let the visitor:
1. See the cut-off range as the default floor.
2. Toggle to avoided-burden and watch the floor drop further (sometimes negative).
3. Toggle to system-expansion / 50-50 and watch the floor rise to where it overlaps with the lower bound of the new-masonry range.

When the rule is system-expansion, reclaimed brick is *not always* lower-carbon than the best new-masonry options — that is the honest finding. When the rule is cut-off, reclaimed brick is the unambiguous winner. The installation that hides this swing is the dishonest installation. The installation that surfaces it is the educational one.

The data in VALUES.md supports both presentations. The installation should default to cut-off (the EPD industry standard) and offer the toggle for the other two rules. The bibliography has the receipts.
