# SYNTHESIS — Strand 02: 3D-Printed Concrete and Earth

## (a) Evidence landscape

The 3D-printed (3DP) construction literature has expanded rapidly between 2018 and 2026, but its evidence base is uneven in three specific ways relevant to this installation. First, the bulk of peer-reviewed life-cycle assessments are wall-element studies — a 1 m² external load-bearing wall is the dominant functional unit. Mohammad, Masad, and Al-Ghamdi's 2020 *Buildings* paper is the canonical wall-element LCA, reporting four scenarios cradle-to-gate for 1 m² of external wall. Alhumayani, Gomaa, Soebarto, and Jabi's 2020 *Journal of Cleaner Production* paper extends this to 3D-printed cob versus 3D-printed concrete, again wall element, again A1–A4 cradle-to-site. Second, the meta-review literature (Motalebi, Khondoker, and Kabir, 2024, *Sustainable Operations and Computers*) is explicit that primary studies converge on the direction (3DCP < OPC concrete in GWP for a wall element) but diverge on magnitude — reduction estimates run from approximately −10 % to −60 % depending on the study, with no Catalonia-specific primary source in the corpus reviewed. Third, the whole-building LCAs that the installation actually needs (kg CO₂ per m² of gross floor area, not per m² of wall) are far rarer; the most-cited are Rossi, Reitemeyer, Heidrich, and Rybski (2024) in *Findings*, the MIT Concrete Sustainability Hub / ICON white paper of March 2024, and the 2026 *Journal of Building Engineering* paper on whole-building 75- and 100-year LCAs across U.S. climate zones. Each of these has structural caveats that are explored below.

The installation's brief — kg CO₂eq per m² of gross floor area, broken down across five phases (foundation, structure, roof, openings, finishing) — runs squarely into this evidence pattern. The 3DP-specific literature concentrates almost all of its quantitative claims in the **structure** phase. The other four phases of a 3DP house are built conventionally: a poured-concrete slab foundation, a conventional roof (usually timber or steel framed), conventional windows and doors slotted into voids left by the printer, and conventional finishing (paint, sometimes skim coat). The VALUES.md table reflects this honestly — `structure` is the phase where 3DP-specific T1 figures exist; `foundation`, `roof`, `openings`, and `finishing` are populated from masonry-strand cross-links with explicit "no 3DP-specific deviation" assumptions.

The vendor literature (ICON, Apis Cor, COBOD/PERI, WASP/TECLA, IAAC/TOVA) is large, accessible, and useful for time and cost figures, but it is also the dominant source of headline numbers that have leaked into popular discourse. Triangulating the vendor numbers against peer-reviewed primary sources is the single most important methodological discipline this strand applies.

## (b) Where the literature converges and where it diverges

The literature **converges** on three points.

1. *Direction of GWP effect at the wall-element level.* Cradle-to-gate, an unreinforced 3D-printed concrete wall has lower global warming potential than a conventional cast-in-place reinforced concrete wall of equivalent load-bearing capacity. Both Mohammad (2020), Alhumayani (2020), and the Motalebi (2024) meta-review confirm this direction.
2. *Cement is the dominant lever.* Across every LCA reviewed, the cement content of the printable mix is the single largest contributor to embodied carbon. The headline GWP advantage of 3DP can be amplified or erased entirely by the cement chemistry choice — high-OPC printable mixes (~30 % cement by mass, ~450 kg CO₂eq/m³) versus geopolymer or LC3 (limestone calcined clay cement, ~250 kg CO₂eq/m³) — a roughly 45 % spread on the same volumetric unit.
3. *3D-printed earth has materially lower GWP than 3D-printed concrete for the wall element.* Alhumayani (2020) reports approximately 80 % lower GWP for cob-based 3DP versus concrete-based 3DP at the wall element. WASP/TECLA and IAAC/TOVA vendor demonstrators are consistent with this direction qualitatively, though no LCA exists for TECLA or TOVA themselves.

The literature **diverges** dramatically on three points — and the divergences are the central drama of "is 3DP greener?".

1. *Magnitude of the GWP advantage.* Mohammad (2020) reports the unreinforced lightweight scenario at −24.6 % GWP versus conventional concrete; Rossi et al. (2024) reports a factor 2.5× advantage at whole-house level (58 vs 147 kg CO₂eq/m² GFA); the MIT/ICON white paper and the 2026 *J. Build. Eng.* paper report a much more modest 2–10 % advantage versus stick-frame and CMU over a 100-year service life. These numbers are not all measuring the same thing — they differ in functional unit (wall vs whole house), system boundary (A1–A3 vs A1–A5 vs full life cycle), reinforcement convention, and reference baseline. The installation must therefore commit to a specific framing in its projection layer rather than picking the most-impressive headline.
2. *Reinforcement boundary.* Mohammad (2020) is the smoking gun: scenario 2 (3DCP **with** conventional steel reinforcement) shows GWP **higher** than the conventional concrete reference. The reduction story holds only when reinforcement is omitted or replaced with low-impact alternatives such as basalt fibre or external post-tensioning. Many vendor numbers and several academic papers do not state their reinforcement convention explicitly. This is the single most likely place the installation's projection could mislead a visitor if the assumption is not surfaced.
3. *Functional unit — wall versus whole building.* The Alhumayani "~80 % cob reduction" figure is wall element only and excludes foundation, roof, and openings. If the visitor sees an 80 % reduction overlaid on a building footprint, the projection is implicitly extending a wall-element claim to a whole-building functional unit. This is methodologically wrong — the building still has a poured-concrete foundation that contributes 25–60 kg CO₂eq/m² GFA, a conventional roof, and conventional openings, none of which benefit from cob.

## (c) The seven methodology wobbles

Each wobble below names a mechanism, gives a magnitude estimate, and points to the VALUES.md row(s) where the projection layer should expose the swing.

1. **Cement content of the printable mix — ±45 %.** A high-OPC printable mix (~30 % cement by mass, ~450 kg CO₂eq/m³) versus an LC3 or geopolymer mix (~250 kg CO₂eq/m³) gives a ~45 % swing on the same wall volume. The COBOD / Heidelberg Materials evoZero claim of "carbon-captured net-zero cement" pushes the lower bound further if validated, but no independent EPD has been located. Affects: VALUES.md `structure / co2_kg_per_m2` rows.

2. **Geometry optimization — 0 to −50 %.** Gangotra et al. (2023) and the Motalebi (2024) review cite theoretical material savings of 30–50 % from lattice infills, hollow walls, and topology-optimized shapes. Built reality is much less generous: the ICON Wolf Ranch and PERI Heidelberg projects use largely flat walls because architectural and code constraints push geometries back toward solid sections. The installation should treat geometry optimization as an "achievable upper bound rarely reached" and use a lower-bound assumption of ~10–15 % material reduction unless the user explicitly chooses a topology-optimized form. Affects: `structure / co2_kg_per_m2`.

3. **Reinforcement boundary — up to +30 %.** Mohammad (2020) scenario 2 shows that adding conventional steel mesh to 3DCP wipes out the GWP advantage and can push it above conventional concrete. Vendor numbers usually exclude rebar; academic numbers vary. The installation's default should be "fibre-reinforced or unreinforced" with the rebar-included case shown as a specific overlay. Affects: `structure / co2_kg_per_m2`.

4. **Printer energy use — typically <5 % but >20 % for cob.** Mohammad (2020) and the broader review literature place printer electricity at well under 5 % of total embodied carbon for high-cement 3DPC, simply because cement dominates. For 3DP-earth (cob, clay), where the binder has near-zero embodied carbon, electricity becomes the dominant remaining contributor. TECLA's reported ~1200 kWh over a 60 m² prototype implies ~20 kWh/m² GFA, or about 6 kg CO₂eq/m² at the EU grid mix average — material, but not headline-shifting against the cement-driven baseline of concrete 3DP. Affects: cross-cutting `printer_electricity_per_m2` row.

5. **Cob / earth biogenic carbon convention — undefined direction.** Alhumayani (2020) treats cob as effectively zero embodied carbon for the earth fraction, which is the standard convention for soil-as-input. This is defensible but not formally an EN 15804 biogenic-carbon-in / biogenic-carbon-out flow. The "~80 %" cob reduction claim is wall-element only and excludes foundation, roof, and openings, all of which remain conventional. Whole-building cob 3DP advantage is therefore much smaller than 80 %, perhaps 20–35 % depending on building geometry. Affects: 3DP-earth `structure` row and the VALUES.md note about wall-vs-GFA conversion.

6. **100-year durability assumption — unverifiable.** The Yang 2026 paper assumes 75- and 100-year service lives for 3DPC walls. No 3DP building is older than ~10 years; durability data does not exist. Vendor claims of "century-scale lifetime" are extrapolations from concrete-cube test data, not whole-building observation. If real-world service life proves shorter, every per-year embodied-carbon claim must be revised upward. The installation should describe 3DP as an emerging method with a durability footnote.

7. **Functional unit confusion — wall vs whole building.** This is the wobble that does the most damage in popular discourse. Headlines frequently cite a wall-element percentage reduction as if it applied to the whole building. The Rossi (2024) 58-vs-147 figure is whole-house; the Alhumayani 80 % is wall only; the Mohammad −24.6 % is wall only; the 2026 *J. Build. Eng.* 2–10 % is whole-house over 100 years. The installation's projection layer must commit to whole-building (m² GFA) framing and be explicit when a wall-element figure has been converted (typical conversion factor wall:GFA ≈ 0.6–0.8). Affects: every `structure / co2_kg_per_m2` row.

## (d) Named biases

Four bias mechanisms deserve explicit disclosure in the installation's projection.

1. **Vendor-funded LCA bias — selection of favourable assumptions.** Many "academic" LCAs in 3DP are funded directly or indirectly by printer manufacturers. The MIT Concrete Sustainability Hub / ICON white paper is the most prominent example: the methodology is sound but the assumptions (wall design, mix choice, reference baseline) are co-developed with the printer manufacturer. This does not invalidate the result, but it means assumptions tend to favour 3DP. Mechanism: assumption-set chosen at the optimistic-but-defensible end. Mitigation: triangulate against non-vendor-funded peer-reviewed sources (Mohammad 2020, Alhumayani 2020), which show smaller and more conditional advantages.

2. **Headline-clustering bias — publication moments.** Vendor announcements at SXSW, World of Concrete, and the Consumer Electronics Show consistently produce dramatic headline numbers (e.g. "house in 24 hours", "$10,000 home", "factor 2.5× lower carbon"). The peer-reviewed literature is far more conservative. Mechanism: media incentives reward dramatic claims, peer review penalizes them. Comparing both is essential — the installation's projection should expose the spread.

3. **Geographic bias — almost no Catalan or Spanish LCA exists.** The 3DP LCA literature is dominated by U.S. (ICON), Northern Europe (COBOD, PERI), Italy (TECLA), Australia (Alhumayani), and Qatar (Mohammad). Catalan figures essentially do not exist outside IAAC's TOVA and TerraPerforma demonstrators, which themselves have no published LCA. The installation projects onto a Barcelona context but draws figures from non-Catalan studies. This must be flagged. Mechanism: research funding follows printer-manufacturer geography.

4. **Survivorship bias — failed prints rarely appear.** Published 3DP LCAs are almost universally of completed, successful demonstrators. Failed prints, scaled-back projects, and abandoned pilots rarely make it into the peer-reviewed corpus. Mohammad (2020) and Alhumayani (2020) both use idealized geometries; the printer-jam, mix-clog, and weather-failure cases are absent. Mechanism: only successful projects are published; the failure-rate denominator is unknown.

## (e) Brief revisit — can the installation defensibly say "3DP produces X kg CO₂/m²"?

**Yes, with caveats.** The installation can defensibly project a range — not a single number — for 3DP walls in the structural phase, anchored on the Mohammad (2020) range of 44–90 kg CO₂eq/m² of wall (or, converted, roughly 30–70 kg CO₂eq/m² GFA assuming a wall:GFA ratio of 0.7) for the unreinforced or fibre-reinforced case. The whole-building Rossi (2024) figure of 58 kg CO₂eq/m² GFA is a defensible whole-house anchor with the explicit caveat that the comparison set is heterogeneous and small-sample. For 3DP-earth, the defensible range is roughly 12–25 kg CO₂eq/m² of wall, anchored on Alhumayani (2020), with the heavy caveat that this is wall element only and the whole-building advantage is much smaller because foundation, roof, and openings remain conventional.

What the installation cannot defensibly say:

- It cannot say "3DP is X % greener than masonry" without naming the cement content, reinforcement boundary, geometry, and functional unit. The reduction figure ranges from −10 % to −60 % depending on these four decisions.
- It cannot use Catalan-specific 3DP figures because none exist. The geographic gap should be visible to the visitor.
- It cannot project TECLA's "near-zero carbon" vendor claim as a defensible number — there is no LCA for TECLA. The defensible 3DP-earth figure is from Alhumayani's cob study, applied with the wall-vs-GFA caveat.

The projection layer's "wobble overlay" is therefore not optional cosmetic candy; it is the methodologically required surface on which the cement-content, reinforcement, and functional-unit decisions become visible to the visitor. This is the method where headline-vs-reality gap is largest, and the installation earns the right to use a 3DP number specifically by exposing the wobbles, not hiding them.

The honest projection: a wide range, narrowed by the visitor's choices about cement, reinforcement, and geometry, with the geographic gap acknowledged and the durability footnote present. The visitor should leave knowing 3DP is *probably* lower-carbon than conventional concrete for the structural phase, *significantly* lower for cob/earth at the wall, *not* yet a Catalan technology with audit-grade local LCA evidence, and that the swing in the headline number is the method's central scientific debate — not a number to be averaged away.
